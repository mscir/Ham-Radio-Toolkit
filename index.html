Field (2 chars)
Two letters (AA–RR). Each field is 20° longitude × 10° latitude. There are 324 fields worldwide.

Square (4 chars)
Field + two digits (00–99). Each square is 2° longitude × 1° latitude. Four characters are commonly used for contest exchanges and station locations.

Square + two letters (aa–xx). Each subsquare is 5′ longitude × 2.5′ latitude. Use it when greater location precision is requested, such as microwave activity, detailed portable-location reporting, or certain VHF/UHF applications.

Common uses: VHF/UHF and microwave contest exchanges, weak-signal digital software such as WSJT-X, satellite tracking and pass prediction, portable-operation planning, station profiles, and awards based on grid contacts such as VUCC. ▸ Understanding the Data — What Does It All Mean? ▾
▸ NOAA Raw Data — Explained
Solar Flux (F10.7)
SFI — 10.7 cm Solar Radio Flux
The SFI measures solar radio emissions at 10.7 cm wavelength, recorded daily by the Dominion Radio Astrophysical Observatory in Canada. It's the single most important number for HF propagation — more solar activity means a more ionized F2 layer, which means better DX. As SFI rises, the Maximum Usable Frequency (MUF) increases, opening the higher HF bands (15m, 12m, 10m).
≤ 80Low solar activity. Only lower bands (40m, 80m, 160m) reliable.
80–110Moderate. 20m usually open. 17m/15m hit-or-miss.
110–150Good. Most HF bands open including 15m and 12m.
≥ 150Excellent. 10m can open worldwide. Strong DX conditions.
Sunspots
SSN — International Sunspot Number
Sunspots are dark, relatively cool areas on the Sun's surface caused by concentrated magnetic field lines. Their count correlates directly with solar activity. Higher SSN generally means higher SFI and better HF propagation. Sunspots follow an approximately 11-year solar cycle. We are currently in Solar Cycle 25, which began in December 2019 and is tracking above initial predictions.
0–30Solar minimum. Poor high-band propagation.
30–80Rising activity. Moderate propagation improving.
80–150Active sun. Good propagation on most bands.
≥ 150Near solar maximum. Excellent DX potential.
K Index / Kp
Planetary Geomagnetic Index (0–9)
The K-index measures geomagnetic disturbance caused by solar wind and coronal mass ejections (CMEs) hitting Earth's magnetic field. It is updated every 3 hours. A disturbed geomagnetic field degrades HF propagation, especially at high latitudes, by increasing D-layer absorption and causing the ionospheric F2 layer to break down. A K-index ≥ 5 is classified as a geomagnetic storm.
0–1Quiet. Excellent HF propagation conditions.
2–3Unsettled. Generally good; minor polar degradation.
4Active. Noticeable HF degradation at higher latitudes.
5–9Storm. Significant to severe HF disruption possible.
Predicted Fredericksburg A
Forecast A-index for Fredericksburg, Virginia
This dashboard displays NOAA SWPC’s predicted Fredericksburg A-index, a forecast-oriented geomagnetic indicator for the Fredericksburg, Virginia magnetometer station. It is useful for anticipating whether geomagnetic conditions may become more disturbed, but it is not the same as the observed global planetary Ap index.

The station A-index is derived from a station’s three-hour magnetic-field disturbance readings and summarizes geomagnetic activity on a linear scale. Higher values generally mean a more disturbed magnetic environment and a greater chance of degraded HF conditions—especially on polar and high-latitude paths. Use it alongside the current planetary Kp value, solar wind, and IMF Bz rather than treating it as a stand-alone propagation prediction.
0–7Quiet to low activity. Little geomagnetic impact expected.
8–15Unsettled. Some degradation is possible.
16–29Active. Moderate storm effects may affect HF paths.
≥ 30Stormy. Significant HF disruption is more likely.
X-Ray Flux
GOES XRS-A (0.05–0.4 nm) & XRS-B (0.1–0.8 nm)
Solar flares are sudden, intense bursts of radiation, classified by their peak X-ray emission as measured by the GOES satellite. The card shows both channels — XRS-A (shorter, harder X-rays) and XRS-B (the channel used for the official A/B/C/M/X flare class). Each class is 10× stronger than the one below it. Large flares can cause immediate HF radio blackouts on the sunlit side of Earth by over-ionizing the D layer. M and X flares may also trigger proton events lasting hours to days.
A / BBackground level. No HF impact.
CMinor. Possible minor HF degradation.
MModerate. HF degradation on sunlit paths.
XIntense. Potential HF blackout, sunlit side.
Solar Wind Speed
km/s — measured at L1 point by ACE/DSCOVR
The solar wind is a continuous stream of charged particles flowing from the Sun. Baseline speed is around 300–500 km/s. After a solar flare or CME, speeds can surge to 700–1000+ km/s, compressing Earth's magnetosphere and triggering geomagnetic storms. Elevated solar wind speed alone isn't always enough — it also depends on the direction of the interplanetary magnetic field (Bz).
< 400Slow/normal. Low geomagnetic impact likely.
400–600Moderate. Watch for rising K-index.
> 600Fast/disturbed. Geomagnetic storm risk elevated.
IMF Bz
Interplanetary Magnetic Field, north/south component (nT)
The IMF Bz is the north-south component of the solar wind's embedded magnetic field, measured in nanoteslas (nT). It is arguably the single most important geomagnetic storm driver. When Bz is southward (negative), it connects with Earth's northward field lines through magnetic reconnection — pumping energy into the magnetosphere and driving storms. Northward (positive) Bz usually means quiet conditions.
> +5 nTNorthward. Magnetosphere protected. Quiet.
−5 to +5Neutral. Watch for changes.
< −10 nTStrongly southward. Storm activity likely.
Proton Density
Solar wind plasma density (protons/cm³) — measured at L1
This is the density of the solar wind plasma itself — how tightly packed the particles streaming past the L1 spacecraft are — not to be confused with the GOES proton flux below, which measures high-energy particle radiation. Density normally runs low and steady; a sharp jump usually marks the leading edge (shock front) of a CME arriving at Earth, often a few hours before geomagnetic effects and Kp rise. It's a useful early warning of incoming disturbance.
≤ 5 p/ccTypical quiet solar wind.
5–10 p/ccElevated. Possible CME sheath or stream interaction region.
> 10 p/ccCompressed/dense. Shock arrival likely — watch Kp and Bz.
GOES Proton Flux
> 10 MeV integral flux, particle flux units (pfu)
Proton events occur when large solar flares or CMEs accelerate protons to near-light speed. When these reach Earth, they can penetrate the polar ionosphere and cause Polar Cap Absorption (PCA) events — complete HF blackouts at high latitudes that can last from hours to days, unrelated to the K-index. NOAA's official Radiation Storm (S-scale) begins at 10 pfu.
< 1 pfuNormal background. No polar cap absorption.
1–10 pfuElevated but below S1 storm threshold. Monitor polar paths.
> 10 pfuS1+ radiation storm. Polar HF paths likely degraded or blocked.
Electron Flux
> 2 MeV integral flux, electrons/cm²·s·sr
This tracks relativistic "killer electrons" in Earth's outer radiation belt, energized by geomagnetic storms and high-speed solar wind streams. They're mainly a satellite hazard — capable of causing deep dielectric charging and component damage on spacecraft — rather than a direct HF propagation factor. For ham radio, sustained high electron flux is a useful side indicator that a storm's aftermath is still active in near-Earth space.
< 500Normal background levels.
500–2,000Enhanced. Post-storm radiation belt energization.
> 2,000High. Elevated satellite charging risk.
▸ Estimated — Explained
Estimated Aurora Activity
0–10 score, computed locally from Kp, Bz, wind speed & A-index
This is not a direct NOAA product — it's a simple 0–10 activity score this dashboard calculates from the current Kp, IMF Bz, solar wind speed, and A-index, combined into a single quick-glance number. It correlates with how far the auroral oval has likely expanded and how strong VHF aurora backscatter conditions are. Treat it as a rough guide, not a forecast — NOAA's Ovation Aurora model is the authoritative source for aurora prediction.
0–3Quiet. Aurora confined to high polar latitudes.
4–6Active. Aurora possible at high latitudes; some VHF aurora scatter.
7–10Strong. Aurora may reach mid-latitudes; HF degraded, VHF aurora mode favorable.
Estimated Aurora Latitude
Approximate geomagnetic latitude of the auroral oval's edge
Derived from the Aurora Activity score above, this estimates how far equatorward the visible/audible aurora boundary has likely pushed. A lower number means a bigger storm — the oval has expanded further from the poles toward mid-latitudes, which is bad news for HF (D-layer absorption, polar path blackouts) but good news if you're chasing VHF aurora contacts or a visual aurora sighting.
≥ 60°NOval contracted near the poles. Quiet conditions.
50–60°NOval expanded. Northern-tier states/provinces may see activity.
< 50°NMajor expansion. Mid-latitude aurora possible; storm underway.
Estimated F2 Crit. Frequency
foF2 (MHz) — estimated from Solar Flux & Kp
The critical frequency (foF2) is the highest frequency that reflects straight back down when beamed vertically into the F2 layer — the ionosphere's natural "ceiling" for that moment. This dashboard estimates it from current solar flux (higher SFI ionizes the F2 layer more, raising foF2) with a penalty applied as Kp rises (geomagnetic disturbance erodes the F2 layer). It's the foundation number used to estimate the MUF below.
< 4 MHzWeak layer. Only low bands likely to skip.
4–6 MHzModerate. Mid HF bands supported.
> 6 MHzStrong layer. Higher HF bands well supported.
Estimated MUF
Maximum Usable Frequency (MHz) — estimated for typical DX paths
The MUF is the highest frequency that will refract back to Earth rather than punch through the F2 layer into space, for a typical oblique (slant-path) DX contact. It's estimated here as foF2 scaled up for oblique incidence (roughly ×2.5, close to the classic MUF(3000)F2 factor used by propagation models), then reduced further if Kp or Bz indicate geomagnetic disturbance. This number directly drives the Best Bands Now recommendation above.
< 10 MHzLow bands only — 40m/80m favored.
10–18 MHzMid bands open — 20m/15m favored.
> 18 MHzHigh bands open — 15m/12m/10m favored.
Estimated Electron Flux
Same GOES > 2 MeV measurement as the NOAA Raw Data card
This card repeats the same raw GOES electron flux reading described under NOAA Raw Data above — it's surfaced again here alongside the other estimated/forecast-style figures so you can scan aurora, F2/MUF, and radiation-belt activity together at a glance without scrolling back and forth. See the Electron Flux card in the NOAA Raw Data section above for the full explanation and thresholds.
Calculated HF Band Conditions
Calculated locally from NOAA SWPC solar indices — updates automatically
The HF band condition predictions are computed estimates based on the current solar indices (especially SFI and K-index) and typical ionospheric behavior for each frequency range. Conditions vary by latitude, time of year, and local geography — these are generalizations for mid-latitude paths. Day and night values differ because the F2 layer's behavior changes dramatically after sunset: lower bands (80m–40m) often improve at night as D-layer absorption drops, while higher bands (17m–10m) may close as the F2 layer recombines. The only sure way to know if a band is open is to listen!
GoodStrong signals expected. DX likely workable with normal power.
FairMarginal. QSB (fading) expected. Some paths may not close.
PoorBand likely unreliable. Heavy absorption or no F2 skip.
VHF Special Propagation
Sporadic-E, Aurora, Meteor Scatter — beyond normal line-of-sight
VHF (50 MHz / 6m and 144 MHz / 2m) normally travels only line-of-sight, but three special propagation mechanisms can extend range dramatically:

Sporadic-E (E-Skip): Unpredictable, dense ionization patches appear in the E layer (~90–120 km altitude), reflecting VHF signals hundreds to thousands of miles. Peaks in late spring/early summer and again in late December. This is the most common form of long-distance VHF propagation for ham operators.

VHF Aurora: During geomagnetic storms, auroral ionization at polar latitudes can scatter 6m and 2m signals over long paths. Signals take on a characteristic "buzz" or "hiss." Requires a K-index ≥ 3–4 and operation toward the auroral zone.

Tropospheric Ducting: Temperature inversions in the lower atmosphere can create "ducts" that guide VHF signals well beyond the horizon — sometimes 1,000+ km. Most common during high-pressure systems in late summer and fall.

▸ GET ON THE AIR
Your First QSO
Step-by-step procedures for making your first contact. Select the type of operation you want to try — don't worry, every ham was new once. The most important step is to key up!
01
Check your equipment
Confirm that your antenna system is appropriate for the band, your radio is set to the correct sideband, and your transmit audio is clean. Start with a modest power level suitable for your rig, antenna, license privileges, and conditions; increase only if necessary.
Use USB on 20m, 17m, 15m, 12m, and 10m. Use LSB on 40m, 80m, and 160m.
Note: The usual HF convention is “40m and below = LSB; 20m and above = USB.” Always operate only where your license privileges allow.
02
Listen before transmitting
Tune around your target band. Listen for a minute. Find a clear frequency with no ongoing QSOs. Check above AND below — a station might be transmitting on the other side of a contact you can only hear one side of.
"Is this frequency in use? [pause 5 seconds] This is KG5NEW."
Note: FCC requires: Always identify with your callsign before or during any transmission.
03
Tune across the band edge slowly
Use the RIT (Receive Incremental Tuning) or just listen carefully. CW QSOs nearby can cause audio artifacts. Make sure the frequency is genuinely clear.
04
Call CQ
Press PTT, pause half a second for the amp/rig to key up, then speak clearly and at a moderate pace. Three rounds of CQ, then your call twice.
"CQ CQ CQ, this is Kilo Golf Five November Whiskey Echo, Kilo Golf Five November Whiskey Echo, calling CQ and listening."
Note: Tip: Saying "CQ DX" limits replies to overseas stations. Just "CQ" invites everyone.
05
Answer a station calling CQ
Tune to their frequency, wait for a pause in their CQ, then call them: their callsign once, your callsign twice (phonetic).
"W1ABC, this is Kilo Golf Five November Whiskey Echo, Kilo Golf Five November Whiskey Echo — over."
Then listen. The calling station will normally come back to you if they copied you.
06
Exchange the basics
For an HF phone QSO, exchange an honest RS signal report, your name, and your location. On phone, the usual report is two numbers: readability from 1–5 and signal strength from 1–9. The “T” in RST refers to CW tone and is normally not used for voice.
"Good morning, you're 59 here in Texas. Name is [name], QTH is [city]. Back to you."
07
Sign off properly
When you are ready to end the contact, thank the other operator, exchange final call signs, and say 73:
"Thanks for the QSO, W1ABC. 73 from KG5NEW."
In the U.S., transmit your assigned call sign at the end of the communication and at least every 10 minutes during a longer contact.
▸ UNDERSTAND & USE REPEATERS
Repeater Quick-Reference
Repeaters extend your range from miles to hundreds of miles. They receive on one frequency (input) and simultaneously retransmit on another (output). You tune to the output frequency.
Standard Frequency Offsets
6m (50 MHz)−1.0 MHz
2m (144 MHz)±600 kHz
1.25m (222 MHz)−1.6 MHz
70cm (440 MHz)±5.0 MHz
33cm (900 MHz)−25 MHz
23cm (1.2 GHz)±12 MHz
Positive (+) offset = you transmit higher than you receive. Negative (−) = you transmit lower. Most modern radios set this automatically from a repeater directory.
These are common North American conventions, not guarantees. Always use the offset and tone listed for the specific repeater.
CTCSS / DCS Tones
Most repeaters require a subaudible tone to open the squelch. Without it, the repeater ignores you — you'll hear it fine but it won't retransmit you.
Common CTCSS tones (Hz):
Do not guess the tone. Look up the repeater’s exact CTCSS tone or DCS code and program it as listed. Your radio may call CTCSS “Tone,” “T-CTCS,” or “PL”; DCS may be called “DCS” or “DCS code.”
DCS (Digital-Coded Squelch) uses a digital data burst instead of an audio tone. Look it up in the repeater directory — set it in your radio exactly as listed.
Find Local Repeaters
RepeaterBook.com — free live directory; search by location, band, mode, frequency, tone, and coverage.
ARRL Repeater Directory — current print directory for U.S. and Canada; a useful offline reference.
QRZ.com — station lookups, club contacts, and additional amateur-radio resources.
Find a nearby repeater, then program its output frequency, offset, and access tone exactly as listed.
How to Use a Repeater — Step by Step Show / hide
1
Program the repeater into your radio
Enter the output frequency (what you tune to), set the correct offset direction and amount, and program the CTCSS/DCS tone as required. Most handhelds let you download repeater files from RepeaterBook.
2
Listen first — is the repeater in use?
Monitor the output frequency for at least 30 seconds. If a QSO is in progress, wait for a pause. Interrupting an existing QSO is poor practice except in an emergency.
3
Test access by identifying
After listening first, briefly transmit your callsign and purpose: “KG5NEW testing” or “KG5NEW monitoring.” Listen for the repeater courtesy tone or tail. Do not key up silently just to see whether the repeater responds.
4
Make a call
Press PTT, wait about one second for the repeater to key up, then say: “KG5NEW monitoring.” To call a specific station, say: “W1ABC, this is KG5NEW.”
5
Leave pauses between transmissions
Wait for the courtesy tone before responding — it signals the repeater's timer reset. This also allows other stations to break in. Long key-ups can time out the repeater and drop you.
6
Sign off properly

Nodes, Linked Repeaters & Reflectors Show / hide

Some repeaters and personal hotspots can use the internet to connect with other amateur-radio stations, repeaters, and shared networks. These gateways are commonly called nodes.

NODE
A gateway that connects a local radio, simplex frequency, repeater, or hotspot to an internet-linked amateur-radio network.
LINKED REPEATER
A local repeater connected to another repeater, node, or network. It may be linked permanently, on a schedule, or temporarily.
REFLECTOR / HUB
A shared meeting point that multiple nodes can join, allowing a regional, national, or worldwide conversation or net.
Common systems: AllStarLink, EchoLink, and IRLP are internet-linked amateur-radio systems. Digital voice systems such as WIRES-X, D-STAR, DMR, and P25 have their own rooms, talkgroups, reflectors, or similar network connections. Before connecting from a repeater: Listen first. Check whether the repeater is busy or already linked. Follow only the local repeater owner's published instructions. If user-controlled linking is permitted, identify with your callsign and briefly announce what you intend to connect to.
Important: Node numbers, DTMF commands, permission levels, timeout rules, and linking policies are local—not universal. Do not use an online command sequence unless the local repeater or node owner explicitly publishes it. Never disconnect or change a connection that someone else established.
Linked-system etiquette
Identify before transmitting. Leave an extra pause after unkeying: linked systems can have a short internet delay, and the pause lets another operator break in. When you finish a connection you started, announce that you are disconnecting and use the local system's published disconnect procedure.

▸ ON-AIR LANGUAGE
Q-Codes, Phonetics, CW & Abbreviations
Practical on-air language for amateur radio: common Q-signals, the international phonetic alphabet, CW/Morse reference, signal reports, and everyday abbreviations. Usage varies by mode, activity, and local operating culture.
QRM
Man-made interference from other stations. "You have QRM" = noise from other transmitters.
QRN
Static/atmospheric noise — lightning crashes, geomagnetic noise. "Heavy QRN tonight."
QRO
Increase transmitter power. "Please QRO" = turn it up.
QRP
Decrease transmitter power, or the low-power (<5W) operating style. "I'm running QRP."
QRQ
Send faster. "QRQ PSE" = please speed up your Morse.
QRS
Send more slowly. Most common CW request for new operators. Always honored.
QRT
Stop transmitting / shut down. "Going QRT" = signing off the air.
QRZ
Who is calling me? Used at the end of a CQ or when you're not sure who called.
QSB
Signal fading (varying strength). "Heavy QSB on 20m tonight."
QSL
I confirm / acknowledge, or the confirmation card sent after a QSO. "QSL?" = did you copy?
QSO
A contact / conversation between two or more stations. "I had a great QSO."
QSY
Change frequency. "Shall we QSY to 14.250?" = let's move.
QTH
Location. "My QTH is Houston, Texas." Essential exchange in every QSO.
QRX
Wait / stand by. "QRX 5 minutes" = I'll be back in 5 minutes.
QTX
Keep your station open for further communication.
QRU
I have nothing for you. No traffic or messages to pass.
QSP
Relay a message to another station on my behalf. "Will you QSP to W1ABC?"
QNI
Net check-in. "QNI" = I am checking into the net.
QNX
Dismissed from net. Net control releases you.
QRV
Ready to operate. "Are you QRV?" = are you ready?
QSK
Break-in CW — full QSK means the other station can interrupt your transmission between dots and dashes.
QRK
Readability of your signal (scale 1–5). Rarely used; RST system more common today.
QNB
Net control. Indicates who is running the net.
QST
General call to all amateurs — ARRL's magazine is named QST. On-air: "QST QST" = attention all stations.
The RST system gives a 3-digit signal report: Readability (1–5), Strength (1–9), Tone (1–9, CW only). A "59" phone report means perfectly readable, extremely strong. CW reports are 3 digits (e.g. 599). Phone is 2 digits (e.g. 59). In contests, almost everyone gives 59(9) regardless of actual conditions — take it with a grain of salt!

▸ Radials & Counterpoises
A quarter-wave vertical is only half of the antenna. Radials or a counterpoise provide its RF-current return path—the effective “other half.” They are not the same thing as an electrical safety or lightning-protection ground.
Ground-Mounted / Surface / Buried Radials
Use these with a vertical mounted at or near ground level. Lay wires on the soil surface or bury them shallowly, spreading them around the antenna base.
Do not tune each radial to resonance. Use as many as practical, distribute them as evenly as possible, and add wire coverage over time. Surface and shallow-buried radials can work similarly; burial mainly protects wires and makes mowing easier.
Elevated Radials / Counterpoise
Use these with a raised ground-plane vertical—on a mast, roof, balcony, or portable support. Start with 3–4 or more equal radials, spaced symmetrically around the antenna base.
Treat elevated radials as active antenna elements: start slightly long near 1/4λ, keep their lengths and geometry equal, and tune the complete installed system with an antenna analyzer. Keep them clear of people, pets, metal, and wiring while transmitting.
Important: A ground rod may be part of electrical safety and lightning/surge protection, but it does not replace antenna radials or a counterpoise. Center-fed dipoles, loops, and beams normally do not use a radial field; they may instead need feedline common-mode control, such as an appropriate current choke.
Starting-length formulas: Half-wave dipole: 468 ÷ frequency (MHz) feet · Quarter-wave radiator: 234 ÷ frequency (MHz) feet · 5/8-wave radiator: 585 ÷ frequency (MHz) feet. Final dimensions vary with wire size, insulation, height, nearby objects, ground system, and feed method.

▸ Grounding, Bonding & Lightning Safety
⚠ Separate—but Related—Station Safety Systems
Electrical safety grounding protects people during an equipment fault. RF return/current control is part of an antenna system—for example, vertical radials or a counterpoise. Lightning and surge protection concerns feedline entry, correctly rated protectors, bonding, and the building grounding-electrode system. These functions overlap in a complete station installation, but they are not interchangeable.
A ground rod does not replace antenna radials or a counterpoise. Do not create an isolated “radio ground”; permanent grounding and surge-protection work must follow local electrical code, equipment instructions, and the requirements of the existing building grounding system.
No grounding system guarantees protection from a direct lightning strike. For permanent installations, consult current code, manufacturer instructions, and a qualified electrician or experienced local amateur-radio installation resource.

Grey Line Map
The grey line (terminator) is the boundary between day and night. Propagation is often enhanced along the grey line — especially on the low bands — as the D-layer (which absorbs HF signals) is absent on the night side but the F-layer remains ionized.
